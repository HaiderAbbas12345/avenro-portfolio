If you have asked a few agencies or freelancers to quote on a new website, you have probably heard very different recommendations. One suggests WordPress. Another suggests Squarespace or Webflow. A third talks about Next.js, React, and a "headless" setup. Each will tell you their choice is the right one.

This article explains what these options actually are, where each one is strong, and where each one creates problems later.

The short version: there is no universally best platform. There is a best fit for your team, your budget, and what you expect the website to do over the next few years.

## What "traditional" website development usually means

When people say "traditional" website development, they usually mean one of two things.

### Theme-based content management systems

WordPress is the best-known example. It is an open-source content management system (CMS) written in PHP. When a visitor requests a page, the server typically runs PHP code, pulls content from a database, applies a theme, and sends back the finished HTML.

WordPress's biggest strength is its ecosystem. There are themes for almost any style and plugins for almost any feature: contact forms, bookings, ecommerce, multilingual content, SEO tools. Non-technical staff can log in, edit pages, and publish blog posts without a developer.

### Hosted site builders

Squarespace, Wix, and Webflow are hosted platforms. You pay a subscription, and the company handles hosting, security patches, and the underlying software. You design pages with a visual editor, often starting from a template.

The trade-off is that you work within the platform's limits. You can usually add integrations and some custom code, but you do not own or control the underlying system.

## What Next.js is, and how a framework-based site differs

Next.js is a React framework for building websites and web applications. React is a JavaScript library for building user interfaces out of reusable pieces called components. Next.js adds the structure around it: routing, data loading, image handling, and several ways to render pages.

A few practical differences stand out compared with a theme-based CMS.

**Pages are built from components.** A header, a pricing table, or a testimonial block is written once and reused. This keeps the site consistent, but a developer builds the building blocks.

**Pages can be pre-rendered.** Next.js supports static generation, where pages are built into finished HTML ahead of time, and server rendering, where pages are generated on request. Pre-rendered pages can be served from a content delivery network (CDN), which stores copies close to visitors around the world. Many marketing sites use a mix: mostly static pages, with server rendering only where content must be fresh.

**Deployment usually happens from Git.** The site's code lives in a version-controlled repository. When a change is approved, the hosting platform builds and publishes the new version automatically. Every change is tracked and reversible.

**Content comes from files or a headless CMS.** Simple sites often store blog posts and pages as Markdown files in the repository. Sites with regular editors usually connect a headless CMS, a content system that stores and manages content but leaves the presentation to the front end. Examples include Sanity, Contentful, and WordPress used in headless mode.

## The trade-offs, factor by factor

### Performance and Core Web Vitals

Core Web Vitals are Google's measurements of how quickly a page shows its main content, how quickly it responds to input, and how much the layout shifts while loading. They are one of many signals in search ranking.

What drives speed is fairly consistent regardless of platform: how much JavaScript and CSS the page loads, how large and well-optimized the images are, how far the server is from the visitor, how many third-party scripts (chat widgets, trackers, ad tags) are added, and whether pages are cached.

Next.js makes some of these easier by default. Pre-rendered pages served from a CDN avoid database work on each request, and the framework includes image optimization. But a Next.js site can still be slow if it ships too much JavaScript or loads heavy scripts. A WordPress site with good hosting, a lean theme, and caching can be fast; one with a heavy page builder and many plugins often is not. Execution matters more than the label.

### Security and maintenance

WordPress core is actively maintained, but most sites depend on a theme and a set of third-party plugins. Each plugin adds capability and also adds code that needs updates. Outdated plugins are a common route for attacks, so a WordPress site needs someone responsible for regular updates, backups, and testing that updates do not break anything.

A pre-rendered Next.js site has a smaller attack surface for the public pages: there is often no database or admin login exposed on the same server. That does not make it maintenance-free. The project still has software dependencies that need updating, and any forms, APIs, or CMS integrations need to be secured.

Hosted builders handle platform security for you, which is a genuine advantage for small teams.

### Editing experience for non-technical staff

This is where WordPress and site builders are strongest. Your marketing team can create pages, change text, and publish posts on their own.

With Next.js, editing depends on how the site is set up. If content lives in Markdown files, editing usually requires a developer or someone comfortable with Git. A headless CMS can close this gap and give editors a friendly interface, but it adds setup cost, another subscription in some cases, and some limits on what editors can change without a developer.

### Flexibility for custom features and future web apps

If the website is likely to grow into something more, such as a customer portal, a quoting tool, a dashboard, or integrations with internal systems, a framework like Next.js gives you more room. The same codebase and skills used for the marketing site can support application features. This is the kind of work that overlaps with [custom web application development](/services/web-applications).

WordPress can be extended with custom plugins, but complex application logic often fits awkwardly inside it. Builders generally reach their limits soonest.

### Cost: upfront and ongoing

Builders have the lowest upfront cost and a predictable monthly fee. WordPress sites range widely: a template-based site can be inexpensive, while a custom theme costs more. Ongoing costs include hosting, premium plugin licenses, and maintenance time.

Next.js sites usually cost more upfront because more is built custom. Hosting for mostly static sites can be modest, but a headless CMS and developer time for content changes can add ongoing cost. For a fuller breakdown, see [How much should a modern business website cost](/blog/how-much-should-a-modern-business-website-cost).

### Hiring, vendor availability, and lock-in

WordPress developers are widely available, which makes it easier to switch providers. React and Next.js developers are also common, though a well-structured codebase matters if someone else needs to take over. With builders, you depend on one company's pricing and features; moving away usually means rebuilding.

Lock-in can also come from an agency. Whatever platform you choose, make sure you own the domain, the hosting account, the code or site export, and the content.

### SEO

Both approaches can do SEO well. Search engines need crawlable HTML, sensible page titles and descriptions, clean URLs, good internal linking, structured data where relevant, fast pages, and useful content. WordPress handles much of this with established plugins. Next.js handles it in code, which offers precise control but requires a developer who knows what to implement. Builders cover the basics but can limit fine-grained control. In practice, the content and the care taken in setup matter more than the platform.

Choose the simplest platform that meets your needs for the next two to three years. Extra capability you never use is just extra cost and maintenance.
</Callout>

## Side-by-side comparison

| Factor | Traditional CMS / builder | Next.js |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Speed | Depends heavily on theme, plugins, and hosting | Pre-rendering and CDN delivery help; still depends on build quality |
| Security | Builders: handled by vendor. WordPress: regular plugin and core updates needed | Smaller public attack surface; dependencies and integrations still need care |
| Editing | Strong, built-in visual editing | Needs a headless CMS for comfortable non-technical editing |
| Custom features | Plugins cover common needs; complex logic is harder | High flexibility; well suited to growing into a web app |
| Upfront cost | Lower, especially with templates or builders | Usually higher |
| Ongoing cost | Subscriptions, plugin licenses, maintenance | Hosting, possible CMS fees, developer time for changes |
| Talent and lock-in | WordPress talent widely available; builders tie you to one vendor | Large developer pool; depends on code quality and ownership |
| SEO | Good with plugins and careful setup | Good with deliberate implementation in code |

## Which should you choose?

### When a hosted builder is the right choice

- You need a small brochure site or portfolio quickly and on a modest budget.
- Your team wants to make all changes without a developer.
- You do not expect complex integrations or custom features.
- You prefer a single monthly fee with hosting and security handled for you.

### When WordPress is the right choice

- You publish content often and want a mature editing experience.
- You need common features, such as events, memberships, or a simple shop, that established plugins already cover.
- You want broad access to developers and the ability to change providers easily.
- You have a plan, and a budget, for ongoing updates and maintenance.

### When Next.js is the right choice

- Performance and a polished, custom user experience are priorities.
- The website needs custom functionality or integrations with other systems.
- You expect the site to grow into a web application, or to share code with one.
- You have development support available, either in-house or through a partner, for ongoing changes.

Whichever direction you lean, the quality of planning and execution will matter more than the platform name. Our article on [what makes a business website actually effective](/blog/what-makes-a-business-website-actually-effective) covers those fundamentals.

### Questions to ask any developer or agency proposing a platform

1. Why is this platform the right fit for our goals, rather than the one you usually build with?
2. Who will update content day to day, and what will that look like for our team?
3. What are the ongoing costs for hosting, licenses, and maintenance in the first three years?
4. Who is responsible for security updates, and how often do they happen?
5. Do we own the domain, hosting account, code, and content outright?
6. If we part ways, how would another developer take over?
7. How will you measure and maintain page speed after launch?
