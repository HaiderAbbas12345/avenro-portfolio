If you have ever asked three people what a website costs, you have probably heard three very different answers. One says a few hundred dollars. Another says a few thousand. A studio sends a proposal for much more than that. None of them are necessarily wrong. They are usually describing different things.

This guide explains why prices vary so much, what actually drives the cost, which option tends to fit which kind of business, and how to compare quotes without getting lost in jargon. The goal is not to give you a price list. It is to help you understand what you are paying for, so you can make a calm, informed decision.

## Why website quotes vary so widely

A "website" can mean almost anything. A single page with your contact details is a website. So is a 40-page site with a booking system, a customer portal, and content in three languages. When two people quote for "a website," they often have very different projects in mind.

Quotes also differ because of who is doing the work and how. A freelancer working from a template, a studio designing from scratch, and a do-it-yourself builder subscription are three separate products. They are priced differently because they include different amounts of thinking, design, writing, testing, and support.

Finally, region matters. Rates in the US, UK, and Western Europe are generally higher than in many other markets, and the same scope of work can be priced quite differently depending on where the team is based.

So the first step is not asking "how much?" It is getting clear on "how much for what?"

## The main cost drivers

Most of the price of a website comes down to a handful of decisions. Knowing them helps you control your budget instead of reacting to it.

### Number and type of pages

A simple brochure site might have five to ten pages: home, about, services, contact, and a few others. Each additional page adds design, content, and testing time. Pages are not all equal, though. A standard text page is quick. A detailed service page with comparisons, FAQs, and custom layouts takes much longer.

### Custom design or a template

A template gives you a pre-built look that you adjust with your logo, colors, and content. It is faster and cheaper. A custom design is created specifically for your business, your audience, and your goals. It costs more because someone is solving design problems rather than filling in a layout. Neither is wrong. The question is whether the template genuinely fits what you need to communicate.

### Content and copywriting

This is the cost people most often underestimate. Someone has to write the words, choose or create images, and make sure everything is accurate. If you supply all of it, the project is cheaper but often slower. If the developer or a copywriter handles it, expect that to be a meaningful part of the budget. Good content usually does more for a site than any visual detail.

### Integrations and features

Booking systems, payment processing, CRM connections, newsletter sign-ups, live chat, client logins, and custom forms all add work. Some are simple plugins or embeds. Others need careful setup, testing, and ongoing care. Anything that handles money or personal data deserves extra attention, and that attention costs time.

### Content management needs

If your team wants to edit pages, publish blog posts, or update prices without calling a developer, you will need a content management system (CMS) set up thoughtfully. That includes deciding what should be editable, building those editing screens, and making sure non-technical staff can use them comfortably.

### SEO, performance, and accessibility

A site that loads quickly, is structured clearly for search engines, and can be used by people with disabilities does not happen by accident. It takes deliberate work: optimized images, clean page structure, proper headings, readable contrast, keyboard navigation, and testing across devices. Cheaper quotes often skip this, and the gap may not be visible until later.

### Migrating from an old site

If you already have a website, moving content, preserving search rankings with proper redirects, and transferring domains or email settings all take time. Migration is easy to overlook in early budgeting and is a common source of surprise costs.

## Your main options, honestly compared

Broadly, small and medium businesses choose between three routes. The figures below are rough, typical ranges commonly quoted in the market. They vary a lot by region, scope, and who you hire, so treat them as orientation, not a price list.

| Option | Typical cost (rough) | Good fit for | Trade-offs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| DIY website builder | Low hundreds of dollars per year in subscriptions | New or very small businesses with simple needs and time to do it themselves | Your time is the real cost; limited flexibility; design can look generic; you may outgrow it |
| Template plus freelancer | Roughly low thousands of dollars | Businesses that want a professional setup without a custom design | Quality varies widely; support after launch may be limited; template constraints remain |
| Studio or agency custom build | Roughly $5,000 to $25,000 or more | Businesses where the website directly drives leads, sales, or trust | Higher upfront cost; takes longer; requires your input on goals and content |

Projects with complex custom functionality, such as client portals, multi-step booking flows, or deep system integrations, typically sit above these ranges.

### DIY builders

Builders have improved a lot. For a new business that needs a basic online presence quickly, they can be a sensible start. The trade-off is that you are the designer, writer, and project manager. Many owners find that the hours add up, and the result rarely stands out.

### Template plus freelancer

A capable freelancer can turn a good template into a solid, professional site at a reasonable price. This works well when your needs are fairly standard. The risks are inconsistent quality and what happens if the freelancer becomes unavailable after launch.

### Studio or agency custom build

A studio typically brings strategy, design, development, and testing together, with a process for each. You pay for that structure. It makes the most sense when your website is a primary way customers find, evaluate, and contact you. ## Ongoing costs people forget

The launch is not the end of the bill. Plan for these from the start:

- **Domain name:** usually around $10 to $20 per year for common extensions.
- **Hosting:** anywhere from free tiers to tens of dollars per month for a typical business site. Higher traffic or complex applications cost more.
- **Maintenance and updates:** software updates, security patches, backups, and small content changes. Some businesses handle this in-house, others pay a monthly retainer or hourly rate.
- **Third-party tools:** booking software, email marketing, form services, and premium plugins often carry their own subscriptions.
- **Content over time:** new pages, blog posts, photos, and seasonal updates.

<Callout title="A useful habit">
When you compare quotes, ask each provider to estimate the yearly running cost as well as the build cost. The cheapest build is not always the cheapest site over three years.
</Callout>

## How to compare quotes

Two proposals with the same headline price can include very different amounts of work. A clear proposal should answer these questions:

- **Scope:** What exactly is being built? What is explicitly not included?
- **Page list:** Which pages will exist, and roughly what goes on each?
- **Content:** Who writes the copy and sources the images? Is editing or proofreading included?
- **Design approach:** Is it a template, a customized template, or an original design? How many revision rounds are included?
- **Features and integrations:** Which tools will be connected, and who pays for their subscriptions?
- **Ownership:** Will you own the domain, the hosting account, and the code? Will you have admin access to everything?
- **Quality work:** Is there any commitment on performance, accessibility, mobile behavior, or basic SEO setup?
- **Timeline:** What are the key milestones, and what do they depend on from your side?
- **Post-launch support:** Is there a warranty period for fixing bugs? What does ongoing support cost?

If a quote does not cover these points, ask. A good provider will be glad to clarify. Vague answers at the proposal stage usually become disagreements later.

## Red flags to watch for

Some warning signs are worth taking seriously:

- **Unusually cheap with no written scope.** A low price is fine. A low price with no detail about what you get is a risk.
- **You do not own your domain or hosting.** Your domain should be registered in your name, on an account you control. The same goes for hosting where possible.
- **Vague deliverables.** Phrases like "a modern, responsive website" without a page list or feature list leave too much open to interpretation.
- **Lock-in.** Proprietary platforms you cannot leave, contracts that make it hard to move your site, or refusal to hand over code and logins.
- **No mention of content.** If nobody has discussed who writes the words, that work will land on you, usually late in the project.
- **No plan after launch.** Every site needs updates. A provider who has not thought about it may not be around when you need them.

## How to decide

Start with what the website needs to do for your business. If it simply needs to confirm you exist and show your contact details, a builder or a simple template site may be enough. If it needs to win trust, generate inquiries, take bookings, or support sales, it is worth investing in planning, content, and solid build quality.

Then set a realistic budget range, including running costs, and share it with the people you are talking to. A good provider will tell you honestly what is achievable within it and where trade-offs make sense. You do not need the most expensive option. You need the one that fits your goals and that you will still be comfortable with in a few years.
